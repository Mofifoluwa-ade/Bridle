Bridle: Technical Spec
Oct 23, 2026 · @Mofifoluwa Adelowo
Overview
Bridle is a NEAR contract that lets an AI agent spend funds on other chains without ever holding a key, within limits the owner signs once.
* Pitch: steer your agents, don't trust them.
* Target: the NEAR "Build Beyond Boundaries" hackathon, build window Nov 1 to Nov 14, 2026. Submissions close Nov 14 at 23:59 UTC. A public repo and an X demo post are required.
* Tracks it fits: agent economy, autonomous payments, cross-chain intents, and private AI if the agent runs in a TEE.
Goals
1. One mandate governs an agent's spending on two chains (one EVM chain and Solana).
2. The agent supplies only four fields. The contract builds every transaction itself.
3. A human veto, with a bond-and-slash incentive for watchers.
4. A 90-second demo in which an adversarial agent fails to drain funds.
Non-goals for v1
* Arbitrary DeFi calls or contract interactions.
* USD-denominated caps. v1 caps are stablecoin amounts, so no price oracle is needed.
* A token.
* More than small test balances before the contract is audited.
Problem and thesis
An agent that holds a key is one prompt injection away from an empty wallet, and an agent behind per-action human approval is not autonomous. Bridle removes the key and keeps the autonomy.
The problem
* Give an agent a key and its worst day costs everything that key can reach.
* Gate every action behind a human and the agent saves no time.
The thesis
* Satoshi: replace trust in a counterparty with cryptographic proof and no trusted third party. Here the counterparty is the agent, so the owner verifies a policy and never has to judge the agent's intentions. Signing authority lives in a contract, and the execute call is open to anyone, so liveness does not depend on one operator.
* Vitalik: "AI as the engine, humans as the steering wheel" is the control model. The agent does the work and a human steers at the edges. "On Collusion" shapes the bond economics, because any reward for catching bad behavior invites a self-dealing attack.
What Bridle does not claim: it does not remove trust in the Chain Signatures MPC network. That assumption is stated in the threat model.
Related reading on vitalik.eth.limo: "AI as the engine, humans as the steering wheel" (2025), "On Collusion" (2019), "What I would love to see in a wallet" (2024).

	Rules the contract enforces
1. Amounts are integers in the stablecoin's smallest unit, so no floating point and no price oracle.
2. An empty recipient list means the agent can spend nothing.
3. Tightening is instant, loosening is delayed. Lowering a cap or removing a recipient takes effect at once. Raising a cap or adding a recipient takes effect after one challenge window, so a stolen owner session cannot widen the leash and drain funds in the same minute.
4. Revoking is instant and freezes every pending proposal.
5. v1 uses a UTC-day counter for the daily cap. A true rolling window is a later upgrade.
Proposal lifecycle
 proposal lifecycle · 8 states, 2 decisions 

proposal lifecycle · 8 states, 2 decisions
Proposals above the cooling threshold wait out the challenge window. A flag pauses them, and a flagged proposal never executes unless the owner dismisses the flag. A confirmed flag slashes the agent's bond. Anyone can call execute once a proposal is signed or its window ends.
Bond and veto economics
The owner is the judge of their own funds. Watchers are alarm bells, and the bond makes both lying and silence cost something.
Mechanics
1. For a proposal above the cooling threshold, the agent's bond is locked.
2. Any watcher can flag it by posting a small flag bond. The owner can also veto directly, for free.
3. A flagged proposal never executes without the owner's dismissal. If the owner stays silent, it expires and the funds stay put. The failure mode is a missed payment, never a lost one.
4. If the owner confirms the flag, the agent's bond is slashed and the proposal is cancelled.
5. If the owner dismisses the flag, the watcher's flag bond is forfeited to the agent and the proposal continues to its window.
Suggested slash split: 40% to the watcher, 30% to the owner, 30% burned. The numbers are a starting point to tune after the first spike.

	Trust assumptions, stated plainly
1. NEAR consensus.
2. The Chain Signatures MPC network, which holds the key shares behind every derived address.
3. 1Click solvers and deposit addresses, for the cross-chain payment path only.
4. The destination chain's RPC, for broadcast liveness only. It cannot move funds.

Build plan
 build plan · 6 phases, 2 milestones 

build plan · 6 phases, 2 milestones
The spike on day one decides the chain plan, and the contract is finished by Nov 8. If the schedule slips, cut in this order: the second chain, the 1Click payment path, the TEE stretch, then the bond economics, keeping a plain owner veto. Never cut the adversarial demo. Submissions close Nov 14 at 23:59 UTC, so aim to submit by midday UTC.
Day-one spike checklist
The spike decides the chain plan: by the end of day one you know whether the contract can sign real transactions on both chains. Time-box it to one working day, and if a step is not passing after 2.5 hours, cut that chain instead of pushing on.
What the spike must settle
* Whether the signer contract can sign for Solana (an ed25519 key) as well as for EVM chains. Community tools suggest it can, but this is unconfirmed against the official docs.
* The correct testnet signer contract address. The community repos I found refer to v1.signer, which may be the mainnet name, so look up the testnet address in the official Chain Signatures docs.
* How long one signature takes, and what deposit or gas the signer call needs.
* That the contract can build valid transaction bytes for each chain without help from the agent.
Before you start (about an hour)
* ☐ NEAR testnet account funded from a faucet, plus the near-cli and a Rust toolchain with cargo-near.
* ☐ Base Sepolia and Solana devnet RPC endpoints, with test ETH, test USDC and devnet SOL.
* ☐ A SPIKE.md file in the repo where you log each result and timing as you go.
Spike steps
* ☐ 1. Contract skeleton. Deploy a near-sdk contract with one view method. Pass: the view call returns on testnet.
* ☐ 2. Find the signer. Read the official docs for the testnet signer address and the sign request format. Pass: you can call it with a test payload and get a response, even an error you understand.
* ☐ 3. Derive an EVM address. Derive a Base Sepolia address from your contract account and a path such as the mandate ID. Pass: the same inputs give the same address twice. Fund it with test ETH and USDC.
* ☐ 4. Sign and send an ERC-20 transfer. The contract builds the transfer from four parameters, requests a signature, and your executor broadcasts it. Pass: the transaction confirms on Base Sepolia with the derived address as sender. Time-box: 2.5 hours.
* ☐ 5. Derive a Solana address. Pass: the signer accepts an ed25519 request for your path and returns an address you can fund with devnet SOL.
* ☐ 6. Sign and send a Solana transfer. The contract builds the transfer message. The executor supplies the recent blockhash as an input, since the contract cannot fetch it, and the message content still comes from the contract. Pass: the transfer confirms on devnet. Time-box: 2.5 hours.
* ☐ 7. Measure. Record signing latency in seconds, any deposit the signer needs, and the gas the contract call used.
* ☐ 8. Policy smoke test. Add one hard-coded cap, then call it with an over-cap amount. Pass: the call is rejected and no signature is requested.

Demo script and submission checklist
The demo's job is to show the leash holding under attack, in about 90 seconds, and the same recording is the required X post.
Demo script
1. 0:00 to 0:15. Write a mandate in one sentence: up to $50 a day, three allowed recipients, pause anything over $20.
2. 0:15 to 0:30. The agent pays a legitimate invoice. The feed shows it approved and an explorer link opens the confirmed transaction.
3. 0:30 to 0:55. Switch on adversarial mode. The agent tries to overspend, then to pay an unknown address, on both chains. Each is blocked on screen, with the exact rule named.
4. 0:55 to 1:15. A large payment queues. A watcher flags it and the owner confirms, and the bond is slashed.
5. 1:15 to 1:30. Hit Revoke and show the agent frozen. Close on the line: steer your agents, don't trust them.

Open questions and post-hackathon path
Six questions are open, and the day-one spike answers the first. The rest are design choices to settle during the build.
Open questions
* ☐ Does the signer support an ed25519 request for Solana on testnet? The spike answers this.
* ☐ Who pays for gas on destination chains: an owner top-up, or a relayer?
* ☐ Does a failed broadcast refund the daily cap counter?
* ☐ Is one agent per mandate enough, or should an owner be able to appoint several?
* ☐ Which agent runtime to use, and is TEE attestation realistic inside 14 days?
* ☐ What slash split works best once there are real flags to observe?
After the hackathon
1. Audit the contract before any real balances.
2. Replace the UTC-day counter with a rolling window.
3. Parse transactions so agents can make DeFi calls, not only transfers.
4. Add USD-denominated caps through a price oracle.
5. Token. Its natural job is the bond asset: agents stake it for credibility and watchers earn it from slashing. Design it after seeing how bonds are used in practice, and do not launch it during judging.
Name checks before announcing Bridle
* ☐ Ticker, so it does not collide with an existing token. Bridle was chosen over Leash because an established LEASH token exists.
* ☐ Domain, X handle, GitHub organization, npm package and a .near account name.
